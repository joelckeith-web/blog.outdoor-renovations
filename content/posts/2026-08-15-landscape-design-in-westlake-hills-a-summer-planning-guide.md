## Why Landscape Design Matters More in Westlake Hills

Westlake Hills sits right where Hill Country limestone meets pockets of Austin's expansive clay. That combination creates real design challenges most cookie-cutter landscapers never deal with. Limestone substrata can sit just below the topsoil, which limits how deep you can dig for footings, planting beds, or drainage lines.

Then there's the clay. Expansive clay soil swells when it's wet and shrinks hard when it's dry. That movement is one of the top reasons patios crack and retaining walls lean over time. A design that doesn't account for this movement is a design that fails within a few years, no matter how nice the materials look on day one.

In Kyle Stoutenger's experience, the most common failure point in Central Texas landscape design isn't the plant choice or the paver pattern. It's skipping the grading and drainage plan before anything else gets designed. Water has to go somewhere. If the design doesn't tell it where, it picks its own path, usually straight toward your foundation.

**Key Takeaway:** Westlake Hills properties need designs built around local soil realities, not generic templates. Limestone limits dig depth, and clay soil movement can crack hardscaping if drainage isn't planned first.

## What Goes Into a Real Landscape Design

A proper landscape design isn't a sketch on a napkin. Our process includes custom to-scale 2D plans, full 3D renderings, and construction-ready documentation your installation crew can actually build from. Here's what that looks like in practice.

**Site analysis first.** We walk the property, check drainage flow, map existing trees, and note sun exposure through the day. In Westlake Hills, this often means working around mature live oaks and cedar elms that shade half the yard by afternoon.

**Soil and grade mapping.** We identify where clay pockets sit versus limestone shelf, since this affects everything from planting depth to where a retaining wall needs engineered footings.

**Zoning by water need.** Plants get grouped by how much water they need, not just how they look together. This is called hydrozoning, and it's the backbone of efficient irrigation design.

**3D renderings.** You get to see the finished look before we break ground. This catches design issues early, when they're a five-minute fix on screen instead of a costly change order mid-project.

One mistake we see often from DIY plans and even some competitors: designing the "pretty" layer first, like plant color and patio shape, before solving drainage and grading. That's backward. Water management has to come first, or the prettiest design in the world won't survive its first heavy rain or its first 100-degree week.

**Key Takeaway:** A complete landscape design includes site analysis, soil mapping, water-need zoning, and 3D renderings, in that order. Skipping drainage planning to focus on aesthetics is a common and costly mistake.

## Designing for Central Texas Summer Heat

This week's forecast has highs near 103°F across the Austin Metro area, with lows only dropping to around 77°F overnight. That kind of heat doesn't just stress your lawn. It stress-tests your entire landscape design.

Here's the science behind it, in plain terms. Plants lose water through their leaves faster than their roots can pull it back in when soil temperatures spike. Shallow-rooted plants suffer first. That's why a good design leans on native and adapted species with deep root systems that can chase moisture further down, even in compacted clay or thin limestone soil.

Native options like Texas sage, flame acanthus, Lindheimer muhly, and Mexican feathergrass handle Central Texas summers because they evolved for exactly this climate. Cedar elm and live oak, already common across Westlake Hills, provide the shade canopy that lowers ground temperature and cuts water loss for everything planted beneath them. The Lady Bird Johnson Wildflower Center is a great resource if you want to dig deeper into native plant selection for this region.

Design also has to plan for irrigation efficiency, not just plant choice. Smart irrigation controllers can reduce water use significantly compared to older timer-based systems, since they adjust watering based on real weather data instead of a fixed schedule. That matters even more with Austin Water's mandatory year-round Stage 1–4 water restrictions, which limit watering days and times regardless of how hot it gets. A design that maps irrigation zones correctly from the start makes compliance easy instead of a constant headache.

If your current system is fighting the heat this week, it's worth checking your controller settings and drip zones now rather than waiting. Our [irrigation & drainage services in Westlake Hills](https://outdoorreno.com/services/irrigation-drainage/westlake-hills) team can inspect existing systems for coverage gaps before they cause real plant loss.

**Key Takeaway:** Summer heat above 100°F exposes weak points in plant selection and irrigation design fast. Native, deep-rooted plants and smart-zoned irrigation are the two biggest defenses against Central Texas summer stress.

## Why Summer Is the Right Time to Plan (Even If You Don't Plant Yet)

Here's something a lot of homeowners get wrong: they think landscape design has to wait until it's "planting season." Actually, summer is one of the smartest times to design, even though we generally pause new plant installation until temperatures ease.

Why? Because summer shows you the truth about your property. You can see exactly which parts of the yard bake in full sun by 2 p.m., where the grass struggles first, and where drainage backs up after a quick storm. That real-world information makes for a far better design than guessing during a mild spring week.

Central Texas has two main planting windows, March through May and September through November. If we design now, your project is construction-ready right as the fall window opens. That's the ideal handoff, and it avoids the rush that happens when everyone tries to book installation crews at the same time in September.

Hardscape design and even some hardscape installation can continue through summer too. The one rule we follow strictly: we avoid pouring concrete when temperatures climb above 95°F, since it cures too fast and unevenly in extreme heat, weakening the slab long-term. Design and planning work, though, isn't weather-dependent at all.

**Key Takeaway:** Summer heat is the best stress-test for your landscape design, revealing sun exposure and drainage issues you can't see in milder months. Planning now sets you up for a smooth fall installation.

## Common Design Mistakes We See in Hill Country Properties

After years working properties across Westlake Hills, Barton Creek, and the greater Hill Country, a few mistakes show up again and again.

**Ignoring root zones near mature trees.** Digging or compacting soil too close to an established oak can stress or kill it over a few seasons. A design has to map root protection zones before any hardscape gets placed nearby.

**Choosing spray irrigation where drip makes more sense.** Spray heads work fine for open lawn areas, but they waste water on planting beds through overspray and evaporation, especially in July heat. Drip irrigation delivers water straight to the root zone, which outperforms spray in beds, slopes, and anywhere water runoff is a concern.

**Picking flagstone without checking the base prep.** Flagstone looks beautiful, but if it's set without a properly compacted base and adequate joint spacing, clay soil movement underneath will heave and crack it within a couple of years. Travertine, by comparison, is often better suited to consistent, engineered patio applications, though it comes at a higher material cost. The right choice depends on your budget, your soil, and how the space gets used. This is exactly the kind of trade-off we walk through during the design phase, before you're committed to a material that fights your soil conditions.

**Underestimating limestone depth.** Some homeowners plan a retaining wall or raised bed assuming standard dig depth, only to hit solid limestone shelf a foot down. This changes footing design and cost, so it needs to be part of the design conversation early, not discovered mid-construction.

**Key Takeaway:** The biggest design mistakes in Hill Country properties involve tree root zones, wrong irrigation type for the application, and underestimating how shallow limestone can sit below the surface.
