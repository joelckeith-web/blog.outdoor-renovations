## Why Fall Is the Right Time to Build in Lakeway

Austin summers can climb past 105°F, and that heat doesn't just make work miserable for crews — it causes real problems with materials. Concrete poured above 95°F can cure too fast, which weakens it and leads to cracking down the road. That's why we avoid pouring concrete once temperatures get that high.

Fall changes everything. With highs topping out in the low 90s and mornings dipping into the 40s and 50s, we get consistent, workable conditions for days in a row. Soil compaction — the process of packing down the base layer under a patio or wall — also goes better when the ground isn't bone dry or flash-flooded. Fall tends to land right in that middle zone.

There's a practical side too. If you want your outdoor kitchen or fire pit ready before the holidays, starting a project in fall gives you a real shot at enjoying it this year instead of waiting through winter. And since Austin's average rainfall sits around 34 inches a year spread unevenly across seasons, fall often brings a calmer stretch before winter's occasional freezes.

**Key Takeaway:** Fall's mild temperatures protect concrete cure times, improve soil compaction, and give crews more workable days in a row — all of which add up to stronger, longer-lasting hardscaping.

## Understanding Lakeway's Soil: Clay, Limestone, and Why It Matters

Lakeway sits in a transition zone. You'll find pockets of expansive black clay, similar to the Houston Black series found in eastern parts of Central Texas, mixed with limestone and caliche bedrock typical of the Hill Country. That combination creates real challenges for hardscaping.

Clay soil swells when it's wet and shrinks when it dries out. That movement is the number one reason patios crack and retaining walls lean over time. If a contractor doesn't dig deep enough or compact the base properly, the ground underneath will keep shifting season after season — and your beautiful new patio will show it within a year or two.

Limestone brings a different issue. It's more stable than clay, but it can sit just a few inches below the surface in parts of Lakeway. That means excavation sometimes requires breaking through rock, which changes both the timeline and the approach for footings on retaining walls or deep posts for pergolas.

In Kyle Stoutenger's experience, the most common failure point in hardscaping isn't the material on top — it's what's underneath. "I've seen beautiful flagstone patios fail within two years because the base wasn't compacted right," Kyle notes from over 16 years of hands-on work across Central Texas properties. "Homeowners blame the stone, but the real problem is always six inches below it."

This is why a proper base — usually crushed limestone road base compacted in layers — matters more than which paver or stone you pick. Skipping this step is the single biggest mistake we see from other contractors and DIY projects alike.

## Patios, Walkways, and Retaining Walls: Material Trade-Offs

Not all hardscape materials perform the same way in Central Texas conditions. Here's how the common options stack up.

**Flagstone vs. Travertine.** Flagstone is natural stone with irregular shapes and rich color variation. It holds up well to temperature swings and foot traffic, and it has a rustic look that fits Hill Country homes. Travertine, a type of quarried limestone, offers a cleaner, more uniform surface and stays noticeably cooler underfoot during hot months — a real plus for pool decks and outdoor kitchens. The trade-off is cost and maintenance; travertine often needs sealing to resist staining, while flagstone is more forgiving.

**Poured concrete vs. pavers.** Poured concrete gives you a seamless surface, but in clay-heavy soil, it's more prone to cracking as the ground shifts. Pavers are individual units set in sand or a compacted base, which means they can flex slightly with ground movement instead of cracking outright. In Lakeway's clay-limestone mix, we often lean toward pavers for long-term durability, especially on larger patios.

**Retaining wall materials.** Segmental block walls are popular for their cost and ease of installation on smaller slopes. Natural stone walls cost more but blend better into Hill Country landscapes and often last longer when built with proper drainage behind them. For taller walls, engineering review becomes important — height and soil load affect what's required by Travis County and local codes, so it's worth a conversation with your contractor early on.

**Key Takeaway:** Material choice matters, but it's secondary to proper base prep and drainage. Pavers generally outperform poured concrete in clay soil, and natural stone retaining walls last longer when built with drainage in mind.

## Drainage: The Part Homeowners Forget

Here's a misconception we run into all the time: homeowners think hardscaping is just about the surface — the stone, the pavers, the finished look. But water management is just as important, maybe more.

A patio or walkway needs to be graded, meaning it has a slight slope, so rain runs away from your house instead of pooling against the foundation. In expansive clay soil, pooling water is especially dangerous because it accelerates the swelling and shrinking cycle that cracks hardscaping in the first place.

Retaining walls need drainage built in behind them too. Without a way for water to escape — usually gravel backfill and a drain pipe — pressure builds up behind the wall. That hydrostatic pressure is one of the top reasons retaining walls fail and lean forward over time.

If your property already struggles with standing water or runoff, pairing your hardscaping project with our [irrigation and drainage services](https://outdoorreno.com/services/irrigation-drainage/lakeway) solves the problem at the source instead of working around it. A French drain — a gravel-filled trench with a perforated pipe — is often the fix for persistent wet spots near patios or walls.

## Common Mistakes We See in DIY and Rushed Hardscaping

A few patterns show up again and again on properties we're called to fix or renovate.

First, skipping a compacted base. It's tempting to save time and money by laying pavers directly on dirt, but this almost guarantees shifting and sinking within a year or two, especially in clay soil.

Second, ignoring slope and grading. Even a beautiful patio becomes a problem if it directs water toward the house instead of away from it.

Third, underestimating retaining wall drainage. A wall without a drain behind it is a wall waiting to fail. We've seen walls built correctly in terms of materials and stacking but doomed from day one because nobody planned for water pressure.

Fourth, mismatching material to use. Smooth polished stone looks great in photos but can turn into a slip hazard around pool decks or outdoor kitchens when wet.

A licensed contractor accounts for all of this before the first shovel goes into the ground. That's the value of experience — not just knowing how to lay stone, but knowing how Central Texas ground behaves underneath it.
