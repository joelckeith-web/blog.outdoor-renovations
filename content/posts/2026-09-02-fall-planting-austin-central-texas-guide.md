## Why Fall Is Austin's Best Planting Window

Spring gets the attention, but fall does more of the work. [Texas A&M AgriLife Extension](https://travis-tx.tamu.edu/about-2/horticulture/monthly-gardening-calendar-for-austin-area/october-gardening-checklist-for-austin-and-central-texas/) calls October the best time to plant new trees and shrubs in Central Texas. The plant spends the cooler months building roots instead of fighting 100-degree heat right out of the ground.

In Austin, that window runs from about September through early December. Soil stays warm enough for root growth long after the air cools down. Fall rain, when it shows up, does a lot of the watering for you. A tree or shrub planted in October gets about five months to establish before the first real heat wave hits in April or May. A tree planted in March gets a few weeks before summer stress sets in.

This matters most for anything with a real root system to grow: trees, large shrubs, and perennial beds. Annual color and turf are more forgiving and can go in almost any time of year with the right water.

**Key Takeaway:** A tree or shrub planted in October gets about five months of cool-weather root growth before summer heat returns. That's a head start spring planting can't match.

## What to Plant This Fall in Central Texas Yards

Fall planting works for nearly everything in a Central Texas landscape. A few categories get the most benefit from the cooler start.

Native and adapted trees and shrubs settle in fastest. Live oaks, Texas mountain laurel, and cenizo are built for this soil and climate. They use the fall-to-spring window to grow the root mass that gets them through their first real Austin summer. Ornamental grasses like Lindheimer muhly and Gulf Coast muhly also do well going in now. They're dormant or slowing down, so transplant stress is lower.

Fall is also the right season for cool-season color beds, shade-tolerant plantings under existing tree canopy, and edible landscaping. Herbs, citrus, and fruit trees all benefit from a season of root establishment before spring growth and fruiting kick in.

## Getting the Soil and Site Ready Before You Plant

Central Texas soil makes planting harder than it looks. Much of the Austin area sits on expansive clay over shallow limestone. Those same conditions shape hardscape and drainage work on these properties, and they affect how a new plant's roots can spread just as much.

Before anything goes in the ground, the hole needs to be wider than the root ball, not deeper. The backfill needs organic matter worked in if the native soil is heavy clay. The finished bed also needs two to three inches of mulch to hold moisture and moderate soil temperature through fall's warm afternoons and cool nights.

Sites with drainage problems, meaning water that pools instead of moving through the soil, need that issue fixed before planting, not after. A new tree sitting in standing water all winter is a tree that won't make it to spring.

## Watering New Plantings Through a Central Texas Fall

Fall rain in Austin is inconsistent. Some years bring steady soaking rain; others stay dry into November. New plantings can't rely on rainfall alone during their first few months in the ground.

Newly installed trees and shrubs typically need deep, slow watering two to three times a week for the first several weeks, tapering as roots establish. Drip irrigation on its own zone is the most reliable way to deliver that water without overwatering the established turf and beds nearby. That's one reason irrigation planning happens alongside softscaping installs rather than after.

This is also where fall irrigation scheduling gets more complicated, not less. As temperatures drop, an established lawn needs far less water than it did in July. New plantings on the same property still need regular attention, though. Running one blanket schedule for the whole yard either drowns the lawn or starves the new plants. ## Fall Planting Timeline: What to Do in September, October, and November

A simple way to plan a fall planting project in Austin.

- **September:** Order or select plants and start soil prep. Amend beds and address any drainage issues.
- **Early to mid-October:** The best window for trees, large shrubs, and ornamental grasses.
- **Late October to November:** Still a strong window for smaller shrubs, perennials, and cool-season color beds.
- **Through the first hard freeze:** Keep new plantings on a consistent watering schedule and hold off on major pruning until late winter.

Fitting a fall planting project into a single weekend rarely works on a property with mature trees and drainage considerations.
