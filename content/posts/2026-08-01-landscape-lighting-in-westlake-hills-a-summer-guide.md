When it's 103°F at 4 p.m., nobody's grilling on the patio. But by 8 or 9 p.m., once the heat breaks a bit, that's prime time for outdoor living in Westlake Hills. A good lighting system is what makes that window usable and safe.

There's a curb appeal angle too. A house with thoughtful lighting on its trees and entry reads as cared-for, even at 10 p.m. when nobody's supposed to be looking. Quality landscaping in general has been shown to add strong value to a home over time, and lighting is one of the more affordable ways to add that polish without a full [landscape design](https://outdoorreno.com/services/landscape-design) overhaul.

**Key Takeaway:** Summer heat pushes outdoor living into the evening hours, and that's exactly when your landscape lighting does its job. In Westlake Hills, where terrain changes are common, lighting isn't just about looks — it's a safety feature for steps, slopes, and pool areas.

## How Landscape Lighting Design Actually Works

A lot of homeowners think landscape lighting means sticking a few solar stake lights along the walkway. That's not design — that's decoration. Real lighting design starts with how you actually use your yard at night, then builds a plan around it.

In Kyle Stoutenger's experience, the biggest mistake he sees from DIY attempts and even some other contractors is over-lighting. People want everything bright, and the yard ends up looking like a parking lot instead of a home. Good lighting design uses contrast — dark space next to lit space — to create depth. You light the tree canopy, the stone wall, the steps. You leave the lawn and other areas dim. That contrast is what makes a yard look layered instead of flat.

There are a few core lighting types we use on almost every project:

- **Path lights** — low fixtures that light walkways at ankle height, spaced to avoid glare into your eyes
- **Well lights** — flush-mounted fixtures that sit in the ground and aim straight up into a tree canopy or against a wall
- **Spotlights** — angled fixtures that wash light across a facade, tree, or piece of stonework
- **Downlights** — mounted higher in trees to mimic natural moonlight falling through branches

The layout depends on your property's terrain. Westlake Hills lots often have mature live oaks and cedar elms with wide canopies, and those trees are some of the best lighting subjects we work with. A single well light aimed up into a live oak canopy at night creates a dramatic look that a dozen path lights never will.

**Key Takeaway:** Good lighting design creates contrast and depth instead of flooding the whole yard with brightness. Mature trees, stone walls, and elevation changes common in Westlake Hills give a lighting designer plenty to work with.

## The Technical Side: Wiring, Transformers, and Fixture Quality

This is where a lot of lighting jobs go wrong, and it's usually invisible until year two or three. Landscape lighting runs on low-voltage current, stepped down from your home's standard power through a transformer. That transformer needs to be sized correctly for the total wattage load of your system, with room to add fixtures later. Undersized transformers cause voltage drop, where fixtures farther from the transformer get noticeably dimmer than ones close to it.

Wire gauge matters just as much. Thinner wire loses voltage over distance faster than heavier gauge wire. On a large Westlake Hills lot with lighting runs stretching across a sloped property, using the wrong gauge means your farthest fixtures might barely glow while the closest ones are fine. A contractor who understands voltage drop calculations designs the wire runs and gauge choices around your property's actual size and layout, not a generic kit.

Fixture housing quality is another area where corners get cut. Cheap composite or thin-plastic housings can crack, fade, or warp after a few summers of direct Central Texas sun exposure and UV load. Brass, copper, and heavy-duty composite housings hold up far better over time and are worth the extra upfront cost. This is a case where paying more once beats replacing fixtures every couple of years.

Wire burial depth and placement around root zones is another detail that separates careful installers from ones who rush the job. Around mature oaks, we hand-dig or use low-impact methods to avoid cutting through major roots, since root damage can stress a tree for years before it shows above ground. This ties directly into the kind of root-aware thinking we bring to [softscaping and planting](https://outdoorreno.com/services/softscaping-planting) work too — the soil and root systems in Westlake Hills' limestone-heavy ground demand a careful hand.

**Key Takeaway:** Voltage drop, wire gauge, and fixture housing quality determine whether your lighting system still looks great in five years or needs constant repairs. These details matter more than the number of fixtures you install.

## Common Misconceptions About Landscape Lighting

**"More lights equal better lighting."** This is probably the most common myth we run into. A yard with 30 fixtures scattered everywhere often looks worse than one with 12 fixtures placed with intention. Light needs dark space around it to have any visual impact.

**"Solar lights are just as good as low-voltage wired systems."** Solar stake lights have their place for a quick, cheap accent, but they can't match the brightness, consistency, or design flexibility of a wired low-voltage system. Solar output also drops in shaded areas, and Westlake Hills' mature tree canopy means a lot of your yard doesn't get full sun exposure anyway.

**"Landscape lighting installation tears up the yard."** Low-voltage wiring is shallow-buried and doesn't require the kind of excavation that hardscaping or drainage work needs. In Kyle Stoutenger's experience, most lighting installs leave minimal disruption to existing lawns and beds when done by someone who knows how to route wire efficiently.

**"You can install it once and forget it."** Bulbs burn out, fixtures shift after freeze-thaw cycles or soil movement from our expansive clay soils, and transformers need occasional checks. A system needs light annual maintenance, similar to how you'd check an [irrigation and drainage](https://outdoorreno.com/services/irrigation-drainage) system before summer.

**Key Takeaway:** The biggest misconceptions about landscape lighting all boil down to "more and cheaper is better." In practice, thoughtful placement and quality fixtures outperform quantity every time.

## Pairing Lighting with Hardscaping and Carpentry Features

Landscape lighting rarely stands alone. It usually gets paired with other features on the property — a stone patio, a retaining wall, a pergola, or a deck. Lighting design should happen alongside these projects, not as an afterthought once the concrete's already poured.

If you're planning a new patio or outdoor kitchen through our [hardscaping](https://outdoorreno.com/services/hardscaping) team, that's the time to run conduit for future lighting, even if you're not ready to install fixtures yet. It's far easier to bury a conduit sleeve under a patio before it's built than to cut into finished hardscape later.

The same goes for [custom carpentry](https://outdoorreno.com/services/custom-carpentry) projects like pergolas and decks. String lighting, post lights, and integrated fixtures look best when they're planned into the structure's design from the start, with wiring routed cleanly through posts and beams instead of stapled on afterward.

Summer happens to be a good season for hardscape planning too, since installation can continue year-round in Central Texas — the only real limit is pouring concrete above 95°F, which we simply schedule around during early morning hours or cooler stretches.

**Key Takeaway:** Plan lighting alongside hardscaping and carpentry projects, not after. Running conduit early saves money and avoids cutting into finished stone or wood later.
